If you've been trading futures and options for a while, you already know the feeling. One good week convinces you that you've "figured it out." Then three bad weeks convince you that you're one trade away from winning it all back. That second feeling is the one that empties provident funds, breaks mutual fund SIPs, and turns friendships into awkward WhatsApp reminders about a loan that hasn't been repaid.

This isn't a story about willpower. It's a story about how the system is actually built — and the numbers, straight from India's own market regulator, are worth sitting with before your next trade.

What SEBI's own data says

The Securities and Exchange Board of India has studied this exact question three times in three years, and the answer hasn't moved much.

93% of individual F&O traders lost money between FY22 and FY24
₹1.8L Cr aggregate losses booked by individual traders over those 3 years
₹28L average loss for the worst-hit 3.5% of traders — about 4 lakh people
75%+ of loss-making traders kept trading anyway, year after year

It gets worse in the most recent data. SEBI's July 2025 update showed individual traders' net losses widened by 41% in FY25 alone, crossing ₹1,05,000 crore in a single year, even as the total number of people trading actually fell. Fewer people are trading — and the ones still in it are losing more per person, not less.

And this isn't a small, careless minority. The same research found that among traders under 30, the group growing fastest as a share of the market, roughly 9 in 10 also lost money. Being young, online, and confident about "reading the charts" doesn't change the odds.

You win once. The exchange, the broker, and the platform get paid on every single trade — win or lose.

Where the money actually goes

This is the part that rarely gets explained in the reels that got you into trading in the first place. F&O trading isn't a closed game between you and "the market." It's a chain of parties, and most of them earn a fixed cut regardless of what happens to your position.

  • The exchange earns a transaction charge on every contract that changes hands — whether that contract made you money or wiped you out.
  • Your broker earns brokerage and other charges per order, per lot, per leg. More trades, more revenue for them, regardless of your result.
  • The government collects Securities Transaction Tax, stamp duty, and GST on the transaction itself — again, independent of your outcome.
  • On the other side of most winning trades sit proprietary trading firms and large institutions running algorithms — SEBI's research found that 96–97% of the profits earned by these professional players came from algorithmic strategies most individual traders have no access to.

How each of these companies actually built that number

Zerodha built India's largest brokerage without spending on ads for years — its revenue comes almost entirely from brokerage on F&O trades (equity delivery is free, but options and futures aren't) plus fees on other services. Its FY25 profit actually fell 23% from the year before, which founder Nithin Kamath himself linked publicly to regulatory curbs on speculative F&O trading and lower trading activity — a fairly direct admission that retail F&O volume is the engine behind the number.

Angel One, one of the older listed brokers and a direct competitor to Zerodha and Groww, still grew profit slightly in FY25 even as trading activity slowed industry-wide — though its own Q4 profit alone fell nearly 49% year-on-year as the tighter F&O rules started biting harder toward the end of the year.

Groww went the opposite direction — profit roughly tripled year-on-year, crossing ₹1,800 crore, on the back of rapid user growth (it's now India's largest broker by active users) right before its November 2025 stock market listing. More users trading, more transactions, more fee income.

None of them need you to win. They need you to keep trading.

That's really the whole story in one line. A brokerage has zero financial interest in whether your specific position goes green or red. Their business model is built entirely on volume — the number of orders placed, not the direction they moved. The more actively you trade, win or lose, the better it is for the company on the other end of your app.

The two stock exchanges behind every trade

Behind every broker sits the actual marketplace where your order gets matched — the National Stock Exchange (NSE) and the Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE). These are stock exchanges, not brokers — you don't have an account with them directly, but every single order Zerodha, Groww, or Angel One places on your behalf runs through one of these two, and each one earns a small transaction charge on it regardless of who wins the trade.

In FY25, the National Stock Exchange reported a net profit of ₹12,188 crore — it's the world's largest derivatives exchange by contract volume — while the Bombay Stock Exchange more than tripled its own annual profit to ₹1,317 crore, largely on the back of its expanding derivatives segment. Neither of them needs you to win either; they earn on the transaction, not the outcome.

None of this requires anyone to cheat you. The structure itself guarantees that a large, fee-collecting machine — brokers and exchanges both — sits between you and every trade, and that machine gets paid first, before you find out if you won or lost.

Why "one more trade" is the trap

The pattern shows up the same way almost every time: a small early win, a growing sense of skill, a loss that feels like bad luck rather than bad odds, and then a series of bigger trades meant to "recover" the loss rather than build on a strategy. SEBI's data on repeat behaviour backs this up directly — more than three out of four people who lost money kept trading in the following period. That's not a market failure. That's the exact psychology gambling is built on: intermittent reward, near-misses that feel like almost-wins, and a sunk cost that keeps growing.

Paid "mentorship" and signal groups often sit downstream of this same psychology. A course fee or subscription is a fixed, guaranteed income for the person selling it — regardless of whether the strategy taught inside it makes you money. That's worth remembering before the next "proven strategy" pitch lands in your inbox.

If you recognise yourself in this This isn't written to shame anyone. If you've pulled from your PF, broken an SIP, or borrowed money to trade, you're not the only one — you're one of lakhs of people SEBI's own numbers describe. The way out starts with stopping the bleeding, not with one more "recovery trade."

If you're already in it — what to actually do

  1. 1
    Stop trading before you plan anything else Not "trade smaller" — stop. Every additional trade is another shot at the same odds that got you here.
  2. 2
    Write down the actual damage in one place Loan amount, PF withdrawn, SIPs broken, money owed to people. You can't rebuild a plan around a number you're avoiding looking at.
  3. 3
    Deal with high-cost debt first A personal loan or borrowed money from a friend usually costs more, in money and in relationships, than any recovery trade could realistically earn back.
  4. 4
    Rebuild PF and SIPs on a fixed monthly amount Even a small, automatic, boring contribution restarts compounding — the only reliable "strategy" retail investors consistently benefit from.
  5. 5
    Talk to someone before the next dip in the account A family member, a fee-only financial advisor, or a debt counsellor. Isolation is what let the losses compound quietly in the first place.

F&O contracts exist for a real purpose — hedging real, underlying exposure for businesses and large investors. That's genuinely different from using them as a way to turn a salary into a jackpot. Nothing about the structure of the market is designed to make that second use case work out for you, and the data above is SEBI's own way of saying so as plainly as a regulator can.
